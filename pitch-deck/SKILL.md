# Pitch deck

Investors spend under three minutes on a deck. In DocSend's data, pre-seed decks that went on to raise got 4+ minutes of reading; decks that didn't got about 90 seconds. A deck's job is to earn that extra time and then the meeting. Design almost never decides it. Clarity and believable evidence do.

Everything below comes from a few sources that agree with each other more than they disagree. `references/sources.md` has the detail and links; read it when you need to cite who said what or go deeper on one area.

## The five things that matter

1. **Clarity over polish.** A stranger should be able to say what the company does after the first slide. Seibel (YC): "You don't want pizzazz."
2. **A thesis, then evidence.** Open with the 3–5 things an investor has to believe to want in, then make every slide prove one of them (Hoffman). Lead with the strongest card and answer the biggest objection early.
3. **Real proof, labelled honestly.** Traction, a specific insight, a bottom-up market. Overclaiming (pilots as contracts, LOIs as revenue) is the fastest way to lose a deal in diligence (Casado, a16z).
4. **Market and why now.** Investors bet on the market more than the product (Andreessen). Show it's big, and name the specific change that just opened it up.
5. **One idea per slide, takeaway in the title.** Big type, no clutter. People remember 5–7 points from a pitch (Hale, YC).

## Figure out what the person needs

Most requests are one of three jobs. Work out which before writing anything.

- **Build a deck** from a description of the company.
- **Review a deck** they already have (a .pptx, PDF, pasted text, or slide-by-slide outline).
- **Sharpen one piece**: the one-liner, the market slide, why now, the ask, the team slide.

Then pin down the context that changes the advice. Ask only for what's missing and actually matters; infer the rest and say what you assumed.

- **Stage**: pre-product, launched, repeatable sales, or scaling. Stage decides what counts as proof (table below). Round labels like "seed" are a weak proxy.
- **Sent or presented**: a deck emailed ahead must stand on its own; a presented deck should be sparse because the founder talks. Often they need both.
- **Strongest card**: traction, a rare insight, or an unusually fitting team. This sets the slide order.
- **Biggest objection**: what will an investor doubt first? Address it in the first few slides.
- **The raise**: amount and what milestones it buys.

| Stage | What the deck has to prove | Doesn't count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Team (pre-product) | Founder-market fit, deep knowledge of the problem | The solution matters less here |
| Product (launched) | Product-market fit: real usage, real decision makers | Prospect quotes, exploratory pilots, one-off deals |
| Repeatable sale | Similar buyers and use cases, growing pipeline | An early burst from friendly accounts |
| Unit economics | Contract value, acquisition cost, net dollar retention, margins | |

## Building a deck

Start from the thesis, not the template. Write the 3–5 beliefs first, then choose slides that prove them. The standard set most good decks converge on:

| # | Slide | What it has to prove | The bar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Title + one-liner | What you do | A stranger can repeat it |
| 2 | Problem | Real pain, and how people cope today | Specific customer, specific pain |
| 3 | Solution / product | What you built and the insight behind it | Product or mockup, not a feature list |
| 4 | Traction | Momentum relative to time and money | Real usage/revenue, honestly labelled |
| 5 | Why now | What changed that makes this possible | A concrete shift with a date or number |
| 6 | Market | How big it can get | Bottom-up: customers × price |
| 7 | Competition | Alternatives, and why you win | Never "no competitors" |
| 8 | Business model | How you make money | One model, one sentence |
| 9 | Unique insight | What you know that others don't | Specific, earned from customers |
| 10 | Team | Why these people for this problem | Founder-market fit, not logos |
| 11 | The ask | Amount and the milestones it reaches | Raise to the next proof point |
| 12 | Vision / close | What this is in five years | End on the thesis, not "Questions?" |

Reorder to put the strongest card in the first three slides. Merge slides when the company is early and has little to say on one. Everything else (financial model, cohorts, detailed unit economics, answers to expected hard questions) goes in an appendix.

For each slide, write:

- **A title that states the takeaway** ("Revenue tripled in six months", not "Revenue"). Someone skimming only titles should get the whole argument.
- **The one piece of evidence** that supports it, and a note on what visual would carry it (a chart with its conclusion in the headline, a simple mockup, a 2x2).
- **Speaker notes** if the deck is presented.

Never invent numbers. If the person hasn't given a metric, leave a clearly marked placeholder (`[MRR, last 6 months]`) and list what they need to fill in. A deck with made-up traction is worse than a deck with gaps, because diligence will find it.

Output format depends on the request. If they want an actual file, use the pptx skill (or a slides artifact if available) and apply the principles here to the content. If they want an outline, give the slide table: title, body, visual, notes.

## Reviewing a deck

Read the whole deck first. If it's a .pptx or PDF, extract the text with the matching skill and look at slide images if you can, since layout problems (tiny text, crammed slides, unreadable screenshots) only show visually.

Then do a three-minute-skim pass the way an investor would: read only titles and the biggest element on each slide. Can you say what the company does, why it'll work, and what they're asking for? That's the first finding.

Structure the review like this:

```
## Verdict
One or two sentences: would this earn a meeting, and the single biggest reason why or why not.

## Top 3 fixes
The changes that would move the needle most, in order. Each with the specific slide and a concrete rewrite.

## Slide by slide
Slide N — [current title]
- What works
- What doesn't, and why an investor would care
- Suggested title / rewrite

## Checklist
Pass / fail against references/checklist.md, with one line each on the failures.
```

Be direct. Founders get polite feedback from everyone; what helps is someone saying which slide loses the reader and why. Give rewrites, not just criticism: a better title, a tighter one-liner, a bottom-up market calculation laid out with the numbers they'd need.

Common problems to look for (more in `references/checklist.md`):

- Can't say what they do in two sentences.
- Thesis buried or missing; team slide first.
- Traction overclaimed or vanity metrics (signups, not usage).
- Top-down TAM ("1% of a $50B market").
- "No competitors", or a competition grid where they win every column.
- Several business models at once.
- Jargon: "platform", "AI-powered", "synergy", "revolutionize", "very".
- Charts with no headline stating the conclusion.
- Text too small to read from the back of a room; crowded slides; busy screenshots.
- Big risk not named; ending on "Questions?"; a table of contents.

## Sharpening one piece

- **One-liner**: two sentences, plain words, what it is and who it's for. Seibel's test: could a smart friend explain it back without asking a question? Offer three versions.
- **Market size**: build it bottom-up. Number of target customers × annual price, shown as a calculation, with the source for each input or a placeholder.
- **Why now**: name the change (a technology getting cheap, a regulation, a behaviour shift, a platform opening) and when it happened. "AI is big" isn't a why-now; "inference cost fell 10× in 18 months, so X became affordable for Y" is.
- **Competition**: real alternatives including "do nothing" and spreadsheets, and one honest reason you win.
- **The ask**: amount, the milestones it reaches, and roughly how long. Tie it to the next round's proof point.
- **Team**: why these founders for this problem. How long they've known each other and who's technical matter more than school names.
