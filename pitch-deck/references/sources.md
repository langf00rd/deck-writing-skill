# Sources and what each one says

Use this when you need to attribute advice or go deeper than SKILL.md. Data points have dates; say so when you quote them.

## How investors read decks — DocSend (Dropbox)

[Pitch Deck Research](https://www.dropbox.com/resources/docsend-pitch-deck-research) (published 2025, much of the data from 2019–2021) and [Q3 2021 interest data](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/docsend-pitch-deck-interest-q3-2021-data-shows-consistent-highs-for-vc-interest).

- Average time on a deck: under 3 minutes (2:44 in Q3 2021, down from 3:10 in 2020).
- Pre-seed decks that raised: 4+ minutes of reading. Decks that didn't: 1:30.
- Typical deck: ~20 slides, ~50 words per slide.
- No successful deck in the dataset had a table of contents.
- Funded decks put product and "why now" earlier, and were far more likely to include a competition slide.
- At seed, the business model slide got the most time.
- Bias: all-female teams got 77% more time on traction, 200% more on market size and 48% more on business model; teams with underrepresented founders got 140% more time on business model. Those slides get checked harder for some founders, so they need to be airtight.

## YC

**Michael Seibel, [How to Pitch Your Company](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-pitch-your-company/) (2016).** Seven questions a pitch answers:

1. What you do: two sentences, plain words, "like baby food". Test by having a friend explain it back.
2. Market size: bottom-up, which forces you to name your customer.
3. Progress: output relative to time spent. Product and customers count; fundraising and BD deals barely do.
4. Unique insight: specific, learned from customers. Passion isn't one.
5. Business model: one; copying the dominant model in your space is fine.
6. Team: number of founders, technical or not, how long you've known each other, full time, equity split. Credentials only if relevant.
7. The ask: say exactly what you want.

Avoid jargon, acronyms, marketing language, and vague words like "platform".

**Kevin Hale, [How to Design a Better Pitch Deck](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck) (2015).** People remember 5–7 ideas, so make each slide carry one.

- Legible: big, bold, high-contrast text near the top. No small grey text.
- Simple: one idea per slide.
- Obvious: understood at a glance. Charts get a headline that states the conclusion. Test: show a stranger; if they can't say the idea, the slide failed.
- Cut animations, transitions, memes, heavy branding, uncaptioned photos, most screenshots (use a simplified list of steps instead).
- "Investors invest in teams, not slides." Slides support the speaker.
- Exception: a deliberately crowded slide can convey complexity (e.g. how many tools a customer juggles today).

**Aaron Harris, [How to Build Your Seed Round Pitch Deck](https://ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck) (2018).** Title slide = name + one line on what you do. Most seed companies have little detail worth showing; keep it short and carried by the narrative.

## a16z

- **Marc Andreessen, [Pmarca Guide to Startups, part 4](https://pmarchive.com/guide_to_startups_part4.html).** Market matters more than team or product: a great market pulls a product out of a startup; nothing saves a market with too few buyers. Quotes Andy Rachleff: lack of market is the #1 company-killer.
- **Martin Casado, [Aligning Startup Metrics with Stage of Maturity](https://a16z.com/aligning-startup-metrics-with-stage-of-maturity-beyond-labels-for-fundraising-rounds/) (2018).** The common failure is signalling more maturity than you can justify. Judge by stage (team → product → repeatable sale → unit economics), not round label. Disclose pivots and slow markets up front.
- **Justine Moore, [The Insider's Guide to Data Rooms](https://a16z.com/the-insiders-guide-to-data-rooms-what-to-know-before-you-raise/).** Minimum deck content: thesis, product vision, competitive landscape, traction, team, rough use of funds. Deck numbers must match the data room.
- **Steve McDermid, [16 Common Questions About Fundraising](https://a16z.com/16-common-questions-about-fundraising/).** Script and rehearse; treat each investor meeting as your only one. The best preparation is running the business well before pitching.
- The partner you meet usually writes an internal memo and sells the deal to colleagues. A deck that's easy to forward and summarize does that work for them.

## Sequoia

[Writing a Business Plan](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/). Ten sections: company purpose (one declarative sentence, mission not features), problem (how it's handled today and why that falls short), solution (the insight, why it lasts), why now (why it wasn't built before), market, competition, business model, team, financials, vision (what you'll have built in five years). Clear thinking and ambition matter more than polish.

## Reid Hoffman

[LinkedIn's Series B Pitch to Greylock](https://reidhoffman.org/linkedin-pitch-to-greylock/). See `examples.md` for the full set of lessons. Key structural ones: open with the investment thesis; steer into the main objection early; name risks with mitigations; numbers must pass the blink test; end on the thesis; keep appendix slides for hard questions.

## Guy Kawasaki

10/20/30 rule: 10 slides, 20 minutes, no font under 30 pt ([summary](https://ahaslides.com/blog/10-20-30-rule-presentations); secondary source). The font rule is the useful one because it makes cramming impossible.
