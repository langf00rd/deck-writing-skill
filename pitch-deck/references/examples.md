# Famous funded decks

They look plain by today's standards. They worked because the business was good and the deck made it easy to see.

## Airbnb seed deck (2008)

Raised $600K. 14 slides: cover, problem, solution, market validation, market size, product, business model, adoption strategy, competition, competitive advantages, team, press, user testimonials, financial. Source: [Slidebean teardown](https://slidebean.com/blog/airbnb-pitch-deck).

What worked:

- **Undeniable proof of demand.** 17,000 weekly temporary-housing listings on Craigslist in San Francisco and New York showed the behaviour already existed.
- **One-line business model.** "We take a 10% commission on each transaction."
- **Benefits over features.** The solution slide led with saving money while traveling.
- **Clear competition chart.** It placed Airbnb as both affordable and online, a corner no competitor held.
- **Three-line problem slide**, one highlighted keyword per line.

The revenue projection ($200M over 2008–2011 at 15% share) was a guess and everyone knew it. The deck won on how simple it made the business look, not the projection.

Lesson to apply: find existing behaviour that proves demand (people already hacking around the problem), and state the business model in one line.

## LinkedIn Series B (2004)

Raised from Greylock with no revenue. Hoffman later [published the deck with notes](https://reidhoffman.org/linkedin-pitch-to-greylock/). His lessons:

1. Open with the investment thesis: the 3–8 beliefs that make someone want to own the company. Not the team slide.
2. Research investors and the funding climate. After the dot-com bust, investors wanted revenue models.
3. Steer into the main objection in the first slides. LinkedIn led with revenue for that reason.
4. One primary business model. Listing several streams looks unfocused (he lists this as one of his own mistakes).
5. Pitch by analogy, don't reason by analogy. eBay, PayPal and Google comparisons made a professional network easy to picture. A bad analogy is worse than none.
6. Show, don't tell. Let investors reach the conclusion from evidence.
7. Name risks proactively with mitigation plans.
8. Name competitors and why you win.
9. Show maneuverability: a main plan plus fallbacks.
10. Be careful with TAM and customer slides. For a consumer product, engagement and revenue metrics beat a TAM slide (another self-identified mistake).
11. Show the product or a mockup.
12. Prefer internal data to anecdotes.
13. Cut qualifiers like "very"; they signal nervousness.
14. Numbers must pass the blink test.
15. End on the thesis, and leave it up during Q&A.
16. Keep appendix slides ready for the hard questions.
17. Choose the right partner, not universal approval.

Lesson to apply: structure the deck as a thesis plus evidence, and put the scariest question on the table yourself.
